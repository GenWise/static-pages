What Gifted Lab is

Gifted Lab is GenWise's programme in Mathematics and Science for advanced learners in Grades 5–8. At Kumarans it runs on Saturdays, on campus, in separate rooms for Grades 5–6 and Grades 7–8. Your child may take Mathematics, Science or both.

It is worth being precise about what advanced means here.

It does not mean

Racing ahead through the school syllabus, or covering Grade 9 material in Grade 7.

It means

Spending sustained time deriving an idea, testing its limits and applying it in unfamiliar situations.

In practice

It can feel slower than conventional instruction, and considerably harder. Children may spend a long time on a single question. That is the point.

A GenWise mentor teaches live by video; a trained facilitator is in the room throughout to run experiments, group work and discussion. Children work mainly with pencil, paper, simple materials and each other — not individually at screens.

There are no examinations, marks or rankings, and no compulsory homework. Between sessions there is about an hour of independent work per subject, most of it open exploration and much of it away from a screen.

What a session actually looks like

Two real Gifted Lab sessions, taught in 2026.

Mathematics, Grades 5–6 — the number machine

The mentor drew a row of empty boxes and placed five dots in the rightmost one. The rule was simple: wherever two dots sat together, they exploded and became one dot in the box to their left. The children worked out that five dots settled into a pattern reading 1-0-1, then tried ten, thirteen and fifteen.

Within thirty minutes the class had moved from calculating examples to finding general rules. One child worked out how to predict, for any number, how many boxes and explosions would be needed, and explained it using powers of two. Another asked what would happen if three dots exploded instead of two. By the end the children had constructed binary and base-three arithmetic for themselves — and only then did anyone introduce those words.

Nobody was given a rule and asked to apply it. They were given a machine, and the number systems came out of investigating how it behaved.

Science, Grades 7–8 — watching ice melt

The first class of a module on Heat and Temperature began with students watching ice melt. For half an hour they placed ice cubes on plates, observed carefully, described what they saw and asked questions — without trying to answer them.

By the end they had made dozens of observations and devised experiments of their own. Ice melted faster with the fan on. It melted more slowly on plastic than on steel. The edges of the cube rounded. One student investigated whether pressure made ice melt faster, and found that rubber bands instead cut through the melting cubes.

The mentor did not begin with a theory or a lecture. She helped students separate observation from explanation, turn their questions into investigations, and reason from what they found.

Is this your child?

Parents often recognise their child in one or more of these.

The child who finishes early

They do well in tests and are rarely stuck, but seldom have to work near the limits of their ability.

The quietly advanced child

They read widely, ask unusually sharp questions or study ahead on their own, but do not draw attention to themselves.

The self-directed child

Already watching science videos and solving puzzles for pleasure, but without a mentor, a coherent sequence, or peers to test ideas against.

The years between ten and fourteen are when children form lasting habits around challenge — whether they avoid difficulty because success has always come easily, attach their confidence to marks, or learn that being stuck is an interesting and necessary part of learning.

How you will know it is working

The absence of examinations and grades raises a fair question. Mentors review and respond to the work children do between sessions. At the end of every module, parents receive a written report describing how their child reasons, what they have made or investigated, how they respond when a problem does not yield, and how they work with others. It is a specific account of your child's learning rather than a score.

For many children the most valuable part of Gifted Lab is being in a room where they are not always the quickest, where being wrong is ordinary, and where other children take unusual questions seriously.

Gifted Lab focuses on sustained mathematical and scientific inquiry. Children explore fewer topics in greater depth, explain their reasoning and test ideas in unfamiliar situations. Sessions do not follow an examination-preparation syllabus. It is not an accelerated syllabus either, and it will not move your child prematurely into higher-grade material.

Allow one Saturday morning, inside school hours on a Saturday the school already runs, plus about an hour of optional exploration per subject between sessions. There are no marks, no ranks, no long examinations and no compulsory homework.

Sessions are recorded so a child who is absent can catch up, and worksheets, slides, articles or links are shared when useful. Children are encouraged to keep their own notes and follow questions that interest them between sessions, but none of that is compulsory homework.
